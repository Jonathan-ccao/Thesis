\section{As-Recieved Membranes}
\subsection{Fouling profiles of the as-received membranes}
\label{sec:res-fouling}
The conditions of the three EoL elements before treatment was established by the Membrane Work autopsies \citep{MembraneWorks2026Mine,MembraneWorks2025Desal}, and summarised in Table~\ref{tab:eol-summary}. All three were lightly fouled, but they differed in the state of their PA layer.

\paragraph{Desal-site first-pass element (4238A).}
\label{sec:red-fouling-4238A}
4238A was in good condition and still met the specifications of the element. Deposits were mainly light iron and aluminosilicate (clay) particles, and FTIR spectrum matched the new element. Dye test showed minor pinholing similar to that of new elements \citep{MembraneWorks2025Desal}. It displayed a lower contact angle, attributed to adsorbed organic or biological material. 

\paragraph{Desal-site second-pass element (4238C).}
\label{sec:res-fouling-4238C}
The main problem with 4238C was damaged to the PA layer, not fouling. Deposits were mainly to patches of iron oxide, some biological material and NaCl crystals. FTIR showed no chemical difference from new element \citep{MembraneWorks2025Desal}. However, SEM from \citet{MembraneWorks2025Desal} showed holes in the PA layer across the surface, which were also seen at higher magnification in this thesis (Figure~\ref{fig:app_sem_4238C_eol}). The dye test confirms this, with dense spots and streaks of dye on the permeate side (Figure~\ref{fig:dye_test_full}). The surface also became less negatively charged and more hydrophillic over service. Similar charge loss had also been reported for membranes cleaned repeatedly with caustic \citep{MembraneWorks2025Desal}, and the pH~10 feed may have had a similar effect.

Only a small amount of foulant could be extracted by acid and caustic, mainly calcium, magnesium and sulphate. These foulant types are consistent with the high calcium and sulphate feed. Almost no iron was extracted, even though both were visible in SEM-EDS, so these deposits resisted acid and caustic. Foulants had also penetrated into the support layers, and scale had left some indentation on the surface. XPS also detected low-level chlorine damage, but the PA layer was otherwise mostly intact. Coupon results were consistently well below the element results reported by \citet{MembraneWorks2025Desal}. Element 4238A met specification with \qty{99.65}{\percent} NaCl rejection, but as a coupon it rejected only \qty{78.1}{\percent}. Element 4238C fell from \qty{97.3}{\percent} to \qty{76.6}{\percent}. Coupon permeability was also about \qtyrange{32}{35}{} times lower than the element values. The elements gave roughly \qty{2.6}{} and \qty{7.2}{LMH.bar^{-1}} at \qty{10}{bar}, whereas the 4238A coupon gave \qty{0.082}{LMH.bar^{-1}}.

Differences between flat-sheet coupon and spiral-wound element performance are well documented. A full-size spiral-wound element is several orders of magnitude larger than a flat-sheet channel and has a far more complex flow geometry \citep{Wei2021}. In a simulated flat-sheet channel, adding a feed spacer doubled the mass transfer coefficient \citep{Wei2021}. Bench-scale coupon tests and pilot-scale spiral-wound tests of the same membrane have also shown different fouling and concentration polarisation, with polarisation \qty{41}{\percent} higher in the spiral-wound unit \citep{Roy2017}.

Absolute coupon values in this work are therefore not compared with element or datasheet values. Every coupon was tested on the same rig under the same conditions, so relative changes in PWP and $B$ between treatment conditions can still be compared.

\section{Cleaning outcomes}
\label{sec:res-cleaning}
This section reports the effect of cleaning on each membrane. Based on the autopsies, cleaning was not expected to repair the PA damage on 4238C. On 4297B, the alkaline step should remove organic and biological material, with EDTA breaking calcium bridges \citep{Ang2006}, and the acid step should remove calcium, magnesium and sulphate scale. The silica and iron oxide foulants were expected to remain. Even a specialist iron cleaner at up to \qty{4}{\percent} did not fully remove iron oxide in Membrane Works' cleaning study \citep{MembraneWorks2026Mine}.
% --------------------------------------------------------------------


\subsection{Visual observations}
\paragraph{Desal-site membrane 4238C and 4238A. } 
Cleaning caused no visible change to both 4238C and 4238A. Before cleaning, the polyamide (feed side) was a shiny, light yellow colour for 4238C, and a darker orange shade for 4238A. The polyester (permeate side) for both was a dull, matte white. After acid and base cleans, the coupon looked exactly the same. 

\paragraph{Mine-site membrane 4297B.}
Cleaning did not noticeably change the colour of 4297B, and the acid and base solutions also stayed clear.

The main observation was delamination along the coupon edges (Figure~\ref{fig:4297B-delam}). In these areas the PA layer had begun to peel away from the polysulfone support and could be lifted off with little force. The damage first appeared after the base clean and recurred in repeated experiments. The autopsy found no delamination on the EoL elements, so cleaning triggered it. The base clean reached pH~11.5, which is above the manufacturer's limit for continuous operation (pH~2--11) but within the short-term (\qty{30}{\minute}) cleaning range (pH~1--13) \citep{MembraneWorks2026Mine}. 4238C showed no delamination under the same conditions. The more likely explanation is therefore that the bond between the PA and the support in 4297B had already weakened during service. The autopsy found foulant inside the support layers, and indentations left by past scale may also have reduced adhesion.

The damaged edges were trimmed off and discarded before testing.

\subsection{Comparison with the as-received membranes}
 \label{sec:res-cleaning-compare}
Cleaning did not chang PWP or rejection significantly (based on Welch's \textit{t}-test, \textit{p} < 0.05). However with such few coupons and wide spread of data, only a very large change would show significant change, so this doesn't mean cleaning did nothing. 

\paragraph{4238A (Commercial clean).}
The commercial clean gave the largest changes, with PWP rising by \qty{142}{\percent} and rejection by \qty{8.8}{pp}. Removing foulants would raise both, since it lowers hydraulic resistance and reduces cake-enhanced concentration polarisation \citep{elimelech2005chemical}. However, the two cleaned coupons varied a lot (C1: \qty{0.147}{LMH.bar^{-1}}, \qty{93.4}{\percent}; C2: \qty{0.249}{LMH.bar^{-1}}, \qty{80.4}{\percent}). Each coupon was also tested in a single run so the improvement is indicative only.

\paragraph{4238C (Extensive clean).}
Excluding C5 and C6, PWP fell by \qty{37}{\percent}, and rejection rose by \qty{7.7}{pp}, both close to but not reaching significance (\textit{p} = 0.086 and 0.069). A fall in PWP is not expected from removing foulants, and little foulants was detected in autopsy (Section~\ref{sec:res-fouling-4238C}). Acid tightening of the PA layer may contribute since acid was the final cleaning step (Section~\ref{sec:lit-cleaning}), but this was reported to have around a \qty{10}{\percent} shift in PWP, and is far smaller than the \qty{37}{\percent} observed \citep{tu2015chemical}. Coupon variation is more likely the cause for this difference. The as-received coupons were consistent with each other, but the cleaned coupons varied 6 times in PWP (Table~\ref{tab:clean-desal-coupons}), and C3 C4 had high rejection, likely coming from less damaged parts of the sheet. Because PWP fell and rejection rose together, $B$ fell by \qty{56}{\percent} ($0.98 \to \qty{0.43}{LMH}$, $p = 0.015$; Table~\ref{tab:ip-B}), however, like the PWP drop, this most likely reflects coupon variability rather than repair. 

\paragraph{4297B (Extensive clean).}
The extensive clean made the smallest difference of the three membranes (PWP \qty{+9}{\percent}, rejection \qty{+1.4}{pp}). This agrees with the autopsy, which did not find much foulants that could be removed with acid and caustic cleans. The change is also similar in size to the effect of cleaning chemicals alone on a clean membrane \citep{tu2015chemical}. Two coupons (C4 and C5) also had higher PWP and lower rejection than the rest, but were not identified as outliers (Appendix~\ref{app:outliers}).

\paragraph{Summary.}
Neither cleaning protocol significantly changed PWP or rejection, and only $B$ on 4238C changed significantly. Changes after IP can therefore be attributed mainly to the new PA layer.

\subsection{Performance after IP (Campaign 2)}
\label{sec:res-ip-perf}
This section presents Campaign~2 first (Figures~\ref{fig:ip-pwp-rej} and~\ref{fig:ip-B}, Tables~\ref{tab:ip-results} and~\ref{tab:ip-B}); Campaign~1 is presented in Section~\ref{sec:res-IP-C1}. In Campaign~2, IP lowered PWP and $B$ by \qtyrange{85}{89}{\percent} on both membranes, while rejection did not change.

Table~\ref{tab:ip-results} compares the IP coupons with the as-recieved and cleaned coupons of each membrane. If IP had formed a new selective PA layer, PWP would fall, as the new layer adds resistance in series with existing membrane, and B would fall by more than PWP, so rejection would rise. 

Because observed rejection depends on water flux as well as on the membrane, PWP and rejection on their own can be misleading when flux changes by an order of magnitude. The salt permeability coefficient $B$ (Equation~\ref{eq:b-methods}) was therefore also calculated for each coupon (Table~\ref{tab:ip-B}).

\paragraph{Desal-site second-pass membrane 4238C.}
IP gave a large and consistent drop in PWP. With the extensive clean before IP, PWP fell from $0.208 \pm 0.011$ to $0.023 \pm \qty{0.013}{LMH.bar^{-1}}$ (\qty{-89}{\percent}, $p < 0.001$). IP step itself reduced PWP by \qty{82}{\percent} ($p = 0.034$), adding a huge resistance to membrane.

The rejection results did not match expectation. With extensive clean, rejection after IP was $76.2 \pm \qty{7.9}{\percent}$, which is the same as the as-received value ($-0.4$ pp, $p =0.92$). Rejection held steady despite a ten-fold fall in flux, because B fell by about the same as PWP (Section~\ref{sec:res-ip-B}).

\paragraph{Mine-site membrane 4297B}
4297B responded the same way as the cleaned 4238C membrane. After extensive clean and IP, PWP fell by \qty{86}{\percent} to $0.046 \pm \qty{0.012}{LMH.bar^{-1}}$ (p = 0.002). Rejection did not change at all, testing at $80.5 \pm \qty{11.1}{\percent}$ after IP against $80.5 \pm \qty{11.6}{\percent}$ after cleaning. Only two coupons were tested, and the large SD comes from a wide gap between them (\qty{88.4}{\percent} and \qty{72.6}{\percent}). So while one coupon showed a  \qty{9.3}{pp} decrease in salt rejection, the other coupon showed a \qty{6.5}{pp} increase. Salt permeability nevertheless fell by \qty{89}{\percent} from the cleaned coupons (Section~\ref{sec:res-ip-B}).



\subsection{Salt permeability (Campaign 2)}
\label{sec:res-ip-B}
Because rejection depends on water flux, it can be misleading when the flux changes so much across coupons. At constant $B$, Equation~\ref{eq:R-sd} predicts that rejection falls as flux falls, because the same salt passage is diluted by less water. If the as-received 4238C membrane (B $\approx$ \qty{0.98}{LMH}, Table~\ref{tab:ip-B}) were run at the post-IP flux of \qty{0.35}{LMH}, its rejection would fall to about $0.35/(0.35+0.98)\approx\qty{26}{\percent}$. The IP coupons rejected \qty{76}{\percent} at that flux, so IP substantially lowered salt permeability. Figure~\ref{fig:ip-B} shows this directly, where after extensive clean and IP, $B$ fell by \qty{89}{\percent} on 4297B (p = 0.11). Table~\ref{tab:ip-B} gives $B$ for every condition.

On 4238C, IP after the extensive clean lowered $B$ by \qty{89}{\percent} from as received ($p < 0.001$), matching the \qty{89}{\percent} fall in PWP, so rejection was unchanged. Against the cleaned coupons, $B$ fell by \qty{75}{\percent} ($p = 0.089$), PWP by \qty{82}{\percent}, and rejection fell by \qty{8.2}{pp}, though not significantly ($p = 0.15$). 4297B behaved in a similar way: $B$ fell by \qtyrange{85}{89}{\percent} and PWP by \qtyrange{86}{87}{\percent}, with no change in rejection. On both elements, the new layer slowed water and salt by about the same fraction, adding resistance but not selectivity.



\subsection{Campaign~1: IP with and without cleaning (Indicative)}
\label{sec:res-IP-C1}
Campaign~1 tested IP both with and without clean beforehand. Results are given in Table~\ref{tab:ip-results-c1a} for 4238A and Table~\ref{tab:ip-results-c1c} for 4238C. Each conditions only has two coupons cut from a single IP trial, therefore the results are reported as indications rather than findings. 

\paragraph{Desal-site first-pass membrane 4238A.}
IP did not lower the PWP of 4238A as it demonstrated in Campaign~2 with other coupons. After commercial clean and IP, PWP was $0.093 \pm \qty{0.012}{LMH.bar^{-1}}$, \qty{14}{\percent} above the as-received coupons ($p = 0.54$) and \qty{53}{\percent} below the cleaned coupons ($p = 0.28$). Without clean, IP gave $0.138 \pm \qty{0.030}{LMH.bar^{-1}}$ (\qty{+68}{\percent}, $p = 0.19$). Rejection was similar for both routes (with or without clean + IP). With clean, the coupon tested at $87.1 \pm \qty{0.7}{\percent}$, and $85.6 \pm \qty{0.8}{\percent}$ without. Both were close to the commercially cleaned coupons (\qty{86.9}{\percent}). Neither methods changed $B$ significantly from the as received membrane ($-45\%$, $p = 0.34$; $-5\%$, $p = 0.89$). There is no evidence of a selectivity gain from IP on this element, and cleaning before IP made no measurable difference.

\paragraph{Desal-site second pass membrane 4238C.}
The no clean + IP of 4238C gave the clearest difference between the IP routes. Compared to the extensive clean + IP coupons from Campaign~2, they had about 2.7 times the PWP ($0.061 \pm 0.010$ against $0.023 \pm \qty{0.013}{LMH.bar^{-1}}$, $p = 0.040$) and higher rejection ($86.9 \pm 0.5$ against $76.2 \pm \qty{7.9}{\percent}$, $+10.7$ pp, $p = 0.073$), while $B$ was similar (0.14 against \qty{0.11}{LMH}, $p = 0.41$). The layer formed without a clean therefore added less resistance to water without letting more salt through.

Against the as-received coupons, IP without a clean lowered PWP by \qty{71}{\percent} ($p = 0.003$) and $B$ by \qty{85}{\percent} ($p < 0.001$), raising rejection by \qty{10.3}{pp} ($p = 0.006$). This is the only condition in the study where $B$ fell by more than PWP, which is what a new selective layer in series with the old is expected to do. The direction agrees with \citet{Ogbe2026}, where IP on EoL memmbranes from a wastewater plant without chemical cleaning raised NaCl rejection from \qty{95.6}{\percent} to \qty{98.7}{\percent}. Their partially chlorine-aged membranes started at \qty{83.9}{\percent} and gained \qty{9.5}{pp} to \qty{93.4}{\percent}.

\paragraph{Limits of Campaign~1 result}
Several factors prevent us from drawing concusions based on this. The comparison is across different campaigns, so there are small differences between coupon handling and testing that impact reliability. Only two coupons were tested, both cut from a single IP run, and their close argreement shows that the IP run was uniform, but does not tell us about repeatability. Campaign~1 PWP baslines were also unreliable for 4238C. Finally, the no clean + IP coupons were not characterised by FTIR or SEM, so a new PA layer could not be confirmed. The result is therefore an indication that IP without clean may suit a new PA layer better than after extensvie clean, and would be worth testing directly (Section~\ref{sec:future-work}).

\section{Film formation on the cleaned EoL membrane}
\label{sec:res-cleaning-effect}
\label{sec:res-film}
The two Campaign~2 membranes started from very different conditions. 4238C had a damaged PA layer with little fouling while 4297B had mostly intact PA layer with iron oxide and silica deposits. After the same extensive clean and IP, both gave the same result. PWP fell by \qty{82}{\percent} and \qty{87}{\percent} relative to the cleaned coupons, and rejection did not rise on either membrane (Table~\ref{tab:ip-results}). 

A PA film in series with the old membrane should have lowered B by more than PWP and raised rejection. Instead, PWP and B lowered by similar amounts. Comparing against cleaned coupons, B fell by \qty{75}{\percent} and \qty{89}{\percent} and PWP by \qty{82}{\percent} and \qty{87}{\percent} (Tables~\ref{tab:ip-results} and~\ref{tab:ip-B}). It added resistance to both, but no selectivity. Section~\ref{sec:substrate} gives us an idea of why this might have happened. The support controls where PA forms, rather than how much \citep{GhoshHoek2009}. The PA can form as a skin over the surface, or penetrate into the openings that supply the MPD, where it adds resistance but leaves gaps for salt \citep{Singh2006}. Thicker PA does not necessarily translate to a more selective memebrane. 

\paragraph{4238C.}
FTIR confirmed that new PA formed (Section~\ref{sec:res-surface}). Since $B$ fell no more than PWP, it most likely did not form a continuous skin. The holes in the 4238C PA layer are much larger than the \qtyrange{10}{20}{\nano\metre} pores of a PSf skin (Figure~\ref{fig:app_sem_4238C_eol}), and would act as large pores during IP. \citet{Singh2006} found that PA formed inside \qty{0.15}{\micro\metre} pores gave only \qtyrange{60}{70}{\percent} NaCl rejection. The as-received 4238C surface was also more hydrophilic than a new element (\qty{34.5}{\degree} against \qty{58.5}{\degree}; Table~\ref{tab:eol-summary}). This could cause surface to hold onto MPD and release it slowly, reacting deep inside the pores.

The cleaning chemistry alone is also unlikely to explain the result. Cleaning did not significantly change PWP or rejection (Section~\ref{sec:res-cleaning-compare}), and the 4238C PA bands stayed close to the EoL values from FTIR. It could however affect the surface the film formed on. The only condition in which $B$ fell by more than PWP was 4238C with no clean before IP (Section~\ref{sec:res-IP-C1}). One explanation is that deposits left on the uncleaned surface partly plugged the holes, giving the new film a tighter base to bridge.

\paragraph{4297B.}
4297B reached the same performance outcome through a different surface. Against the cleaned coupons, PWP fell by \qty{87}{\percent} and $B$ by \qty{89}{\percent}, with no change in rejection. Unlike 4238C, the PA bands did not increase after IP (Table~\ref{tab:ftir_ratios}), and the iron oxide and silica deposits remained (Figure~\ref{fig:sem_4297B}c). The residual deposits most likely caused this. They produce uneven films and can leave unsealed gaps. SEM agrees, showing a much less uniform layer on 4238C. 

\paragraph{Why neither membrane gains selectivity.}
On both membrane, salt passage remained controlled byu the aged membrane. Two explanations fit this data:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{PA lined defects and did not bridge them.} This fits 4238C best. No FTIR confirms new PA, and the spread in rejection follows the uneven damage.
  \item \textbf{A thin or patchy film below FTIR detection.} This fits 4297B best. No FTIR change, deposits still present, and a less uniform layer in SEM.
\end{enumerate}


\section{Limitations and sources of error}
\label{sec:res-limitations}
The main constraints are explained below, in order of likeliness. 

\paragraph{Rig limitation.}
Coupons rejected far less than their element counterparts. Without a new-membrane control or blank cell, we cannot ignore the possibility of leaks in the rig lowering coupon rejection or PWP. 

\paragraph{Replication and sampling.}
All coupons for a membrane came from one sheet of one element, so they repicate the sheet rather than the full module. Several conditions had $n = 2$, and each IP condition came from a single IP run. Coupons were also cut at similar places rather than randomised across module. 

\paragraph{Cleaning protocol.}
The base clean held pH~11.5 for \qty{24}{\hour}, well beyond the \qty{30}{\minute} short-term limit. It may have altered the PA or support, as the edge delamination of 4297B suggests. Results may not represent standard cleaning practices.

\paragraph{Cross-campaign comparison.}
Campaign~1 and Campaign~2 differed in coupon handling and testing, and Campaign~1 PWP was unreplicable for 4238C. All Campaign~1 IP coupons fell between \qty{85}{\percent} and \qty{88.7}{\percent} rejection, which may reflect a bias in the campaign rather than treatment and would warrent further investigation, 

\paragraph{Characterisation.}
ATR-FTIR samples mostly the support, so a thin or patchy film may go undetected. The no-clean + IP were also not characterised, and contact angle and zeta potential were not measured. Changes in surface chemistry that would affect film formation therefore were not quantified. 

\paragraph{Generalisation.}
Only three elements were tested. The results describe these particular membranes, and likely does not directly translate to other foulant types of damage mechanisms.
