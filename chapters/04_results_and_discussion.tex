\section{Fouling profiles of the as-received membranes}
\label{sec:res-fouling}
Reports what was actually on membranes A and C before any treatment, since every later result is conditioned on this starting state.

\begin{itemize}
  \item FTIR and SEM-EDS evidence per membrane; dominant foulant classes and their elemental signatures.
  \item Coverage and heterogeneity across the coupon; note any position dependence.
  \item Contrast A versus C and relate to first- versus second-pass duty.
  \item Interpret against the foulant classes in Section~\ref{sec:fouling-ip} --- which mechanisms in that section are expected to be active here.
\end{itemize}


\section{Cleaning outcomes}
\label{sec:res-cleaning}
Shows what the extensive clean actually removed, establishing the two substrate conditions that IP was performed on.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Visual and observational effects of the clean, against the uncleaned control.
  \item Where available, post-clean FTIR/SEM-EDS versus the as-received baseline in Section~\ref{sec:res-fouling}.
  \item Assess whether the extensive clean visibly removed the fouling layer, or left the residuals predicted in Section~\ref{sec:fouling-ip}.
\end{itemize}


\section{Pure water permeability}
\label{sec:res-pwp}
Presents PWP across arms and membranes, before and after IP, to show the densification the new polyamide layer produces.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Figure --- $x$ = membrane/condition (e.g.\ 4238A no clean, clean); two bars each (IP versus no IP); $y$ = average permeability (LMH/bar) with error bars from trial spread.
  \item Expected trend --- IP densifies the surface and reduces permeability.
  \item Observed example --- 4238C falls from 0.92 to 0.52\,LMH/bar after IP.
  \item Compare against new-membrane and EoL baselines.
\end{itemize}


\section{Salt rejection}
\label{sec:res-rejection}
Presents NaCl rejection across arms and membranes, which is the direct test of whether selectivity was restored.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Figure --- $y$ = average rejection (\%), same grouping as Section~\ref{sec:res-pwp}.
  \item Expected trend --- IP recovers rejection on degraded membranes.
  \item Treat replicate scatter as data, not noise: high variance is a predicted signature of defect-driven films (Section~\ref{sec:fouling-ip}).
\end{itemize}


\section{Permeability--rejection trade-off}
\label{sec:res-tradeoff}
Places the regenerated coupons on the permeability--rejection plane against commercial RO, which is the headline result of the thesis.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Figure --- $x$ = permeability (LMH/bar), $y$ = rejection (\%).
  \item Overlay reference points: new commercial RO, the EoL membranes before treatment, transformed NF/UF from the literature.
  \item State plainly whether regenerated coupons land in the commercial RO region.
\end{itemize}


\section{Effect of cleaning on regeneration}
\label{sec:res-cleaning-effect}
Answers the central question of the thesis: whether the substrate must be cleaned before IP for regeneration to work at all.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Arm 1 (no clean) versus Arm 2 (extensive clean), for both membranes.
  \item Test the necessity argument from Section~\ref{sec:substrate-prep}: an uncleaned substrate should yield a defective film --- high permeability, low rejection, wide replicate scatter.
  \item Test the sufficiency question separately: does the cleaned arm reach commercial RO territory, or only improve on the uncleaned one? These are different claims and the distinction matters.
  \item Distinguish a real effect from replicate scatter; state the limits of what this replicate count can support.
  \item If the cleaned arm does \emph{not} deliver the expected permeability drop and rejection gain, that is a reportable result --- interpret it against the five mechanisms in Section~\ref{sec:fouling-ip} rather than treating it as a failed experiment.
\end{itemize}


\section{Membrane A versus membrane C}
\label{sec:res-a-vs-c}
Attributes the differences between the two membranes to their fouling profiles and service history rather than to the regeneration protocol.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Differences in response attributable to first- versus second-pass origin.
  \item Link back to the fouling profiles in Section~\ref{sec:res-fouling}: does fouling type predict regeneration outcome better than cleaning does?
\end{itemize}


\section{Autopsy findings}
\label{sec:res-autopsy}
Presents the surface chemistry and morphology of regenerated coupons as physical evidence for the performance story above.

\begin{itemize}
  \item FTIR/XPS confirmation that a polyamide layer formed; amide band recovery.
  \item SEM morphology --- ridge-and-valley structure, coverage, visible defects or delamination.
  \item Pair a high-performing and a low-performing coupon and explain the difference mechanistically.
\end{itemize}


\section{Discussion synthesis}
\label{sec:res-synthesis}
Reconciles the results with Chapter~\ref{ch:literature_review}, stating what worked, what did not, and which mechanism best explains the data.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Which of the fouling-interference mechanisms in Section~\ref{sec:fouling-ip} the data supports, and which it rules out.
  \item Position the results against reported regeneration performance (Section~\ref{sec:uplifting}).
  \item Answer each research gap from Section~\ref{sec:research-gap} explicitly.
  \item Note anything genuinely unexpected and resist over-explaining it.
\end{itemize}


\section{Limitations and sources of error}
\label{sec:res-limitations}
States the constraints on how far these results can be generalised, and where the largest uncertainties sit.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Replicate count, coupon cutting, rig variability, measurement uncertainty.
  \item IP variability and cleaning variability between runs.
  \item Compaction duration; single feed chemistry; coupon rather than module scale.
\end{itemize}

\end{itemize}
