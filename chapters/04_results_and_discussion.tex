\section{Fouling profiles of the as-received membranes}
\label{sec:res-fouling}
This section reports the condition of the two EoL membrane elements before any treatment, as determined by the Membrane Works autopsies conducted \citep{MembraneWorks2025Desal,MembraneWorks2026Mine}. … All three elements were lightly fouled. 4238A, the first-pass desal element, was in good condition and still performed to specification. 4238C, the second-pass desal element, mainly showed damage to the PA layer with loss of separation performance. 4297B had light particle and biological fouling, but its PA layer was mostly intact.

\subsection{Desal-site first-pass element (4238A)}
\label{sec:red-fouling-4238A}
\paragraph{Desposits.}
4238A was in good condition. A very light brown fouling layer was visible when membrane was wiped, and the membrane had a yellow-to-pink colour that did not wipe off. The brine seal and permeate tube were clean, there was no signs of delamination or backpressure damage, and permeate side was clean \citep{MembraneWorks2025Desal}.

SEM-EDS found iron mixed with aluminosilicate (clay) particles, up to about 13 at.\% Fe, 12 at.\% Si and 11 at.\% Al. Regions without deposits showed mainly C, O and S from the polysulfone support. The FTIR spectra of used 4238A and new 4238B were very similar.

\paragraph{PA layer condition.}
4238A still met its specification as an element: normalised flux was \qty{26.6}{LMH} against \qty{22.1}{LMH} expected, and salt rejection was \qty{99.65}{\percent} against \qty{99.68}{\percent} expected \citep{MembraneWorks2025Desal}. In the dye test it showed only minor pinholing along the lines of brine-spacer contact, similar to the new elements, with \qty{91}{\percent} rejection in the dye cell. Its contact angle (\qty{25.1}{\degree} against \qty{44.2}{\degree} for new 4238B) and zeta potential at pH~7 (\qty{-30}{mV} against \qty{-8}{mV}) were both different from the new element. Membrane Works attributed the lower contact angle to adsorbed organic or biological material \citep{MembraneWorks2025Desal}.

\subsection{Desal-site second-pass element (4238C)}
\label{sec:res-fouling-4238C}

\paragraph{Deposits.} 
The 4238C membrane was visually clean, with a slight yellow colour. Wiping and scraping the surface removed a small amount of brown deposit. The end caps, brine seal and permeate tube were clean, and there was no damage on the permeate side \citep{MembraneWorks2025Desal}. 

SEM-EDS found three types of deposits:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Iron oxide was found in patches. Some regions showed up to 30.9\% Fe with small amounts of C, S, Al and Si. 
  \item Biological materials. Regions contain up to 14.3\% of nitrogen content. These also contained adsorbed inorganic salts.
  \item Sodium chloride crystals (45.4\% Na, 38.7\% Cl).
\end{itemize}
Areas without any deposits comprised mainly of C, O and S, which comes from the polysulfone support layer. 

The ATR-FTIR spectra of 4238C, and the brand new 4238D were very similar, and no significant chemical difference between the new and used membranes were detected \citep{MembraneWorks2025Desal}. 

\paragraph{PA layer condition}
The main problem with 4238C was damage to the PA layer, not fouling. SEM showed holes in the PA layer across the surface, and energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) couldn't identify a cause. Similar but smaller holes were also observed on the unused 4238D.

The dye test also supports this. Of the four desal site membranes tested, 4238C let the most dye through, with dense spots and streaks of dye visible across its permeate side (Figures~\ref{fig:dye_test_full} and~\ref{fig:dye_test_magnified}). Its permeate conductivity during the dye test was \qty{113}{\micro\siemens\per\centi\metre}, compared with around \qtyrange{20}{30}{\micro\siemens\per\centi\metre} for the others. By comparison, the used first-pass element 4238A showed low dye penetration, similar to both new elements (\qty{91}{\percent} rejection in the dye cell).More dye also stuck to the feed side of 4238C, which can indicate fouling or surface damage (Figure~\ref{fig:dye_test_full}).

\input{chapters/dye_test_figures}

Standard performance testing by \citet{MembraneWorks2025Desal} on a single 8" element showed the effect of the membrane damage. The test used a feed salt concentration of \qty{1500}{\milli\gram\per\litre} at a pressure of \qty{10}{bar}. The 4238C EoL membrane had a flux of \qty{71.9}{LMH}, about twice the expected \qty{37.3}{LMH}, and a salt rejection of \qty{97.3}{\percent}, below the expected \qty{99.46}{\percent}. High flux combined with low rejection indicates that the PA layer may be chemically or physically damaged.

\flag{EXPLAIN}
The surface properties of 4238C had also changed compared to the new 4238D, even though FTIR showed no chemical difference. The isoelectric point increased from pH~3.1 to 3.6; The zeta potential at pH~7 became less negative, from \qty{-42}{mV} to \qty{-16}{mV}; the water contact angle decreased from \qty{58.5}{\degree} to \qty{34.5}{\degree}, meaning the surface became more hydrophilic.

The lower contact angle could be attributed to organic or biological material adsorbed onto the surface, and the loss of negative charge is similar to what has been reported for membranes cleaned repeatedly with caustic \citep{MembraneWorks2025Desal}. The pH~10 feed to the second pass may have had a similar effect. A less negative surface repels borate ions less strongly, which is consistent with the falling boron rejection reported by the plant.


\subsection{Mine-site element (4297B)}
\paragraph{Deposits.}
The mine-site feed water is high in calcium and sulphates. Using water chemistry modelling, calcium sulphate (gypsum) and silica were the main scaling risk in the feed for this membrane \citep{MembraneWorks2026Mine}. The 4297B membrane had a light brown fouling layer across the surface. Both end caps had spots of brown fouling but no debris, and brine spacer was clean. 

Although there was significant visible fouling on the membrane, fouling analysis by extraction was conducted and showed that the amount of extractable foulant was low. Around \qty{237.5}{mg.m^{-2}} with similar composition across the element: 

\begin{itemize}
  \item calcium: \qtyrange{91}{92}{mg.m^{-2}};
  \item carbon: \qtyrange{69}{75}{mg.m^{-2}};
  \item magnesium: \qtyrange{30}{31}{mg.m^{-2}};
  \item sulfur, likely as sulphate: \qtyrange{26}{27}{mg.m^{-2}};
  \item silicon: \qtyrange{10}{11}{mg.m^{-2}};
  \item iron: \qty{1}{mg.m^{-2}} or less.
\end{itemize}

SEM-EDS showed particles scattered across the surface of the membrane. Analysis detected iron oxide (37.3 \% Fe and 50.7\% O, with small amounts of Al, S and Si (2.8, 2.38, 1.81\% respectively)) in some regions, as well as silica (29.6\% Si and 65.8\% O) in others. A low level of nitrogen (5.4 \%) was also seen across the surface, indicating a small amount of biofouling. 

While Iron was visible in the SEM-EDS, almost non was recovered by extraction, suggesting that iron oxide was not easily dissolved by acid or caustic. This is discussed further in Section~\ref{sec:res-cleaning}

4297B also showed more signs of biological fouling than 4238C. Astra Blue staining showed brown fouling under the brine spacer, attributed to extracellular polymeric substance. The bacterial growth count was \qtyrange{5800}{7800}{CFU/mL} classifying as moderate growth \citep{MembraneWorks2026Mine}.

\paragraph{PA layer condition.}
A swab of the permeate tube showed slight fouling and the back of the membrane was stained brown. Under the optical microscope, the foulant appeared to have penetrated into the support layers. SEM also showed indentations in the surface where the scale had previously been. 

The PA layer of 4297B showed minor chlorine damage, with a negative Fujiwara test (which determines polyamide RO membrane exposure to oxidising halogens). XPS detected \qty{0.2}{\percent} organic chlorine, indicating some low level chlorine damage is present. Incorrect sodium metabisulfite (SMBS) dosing can react with iron on the membrane and catalyse SMBS degradation and cause oxidative damage \citep{MembraneWorks2025Desal}.

Performance of the 4297B membrane was not tested by Membrane Works, and the reported drop to around \qty{85}{\percent} rejection was measured for the whole RO train. The autopsy report concluded that the fouling and damage did not fully explain this drop.

The damage to 4238C therefore cannot be attributed to the age of the plant or the plant's feed water. It is more likely linked to its second-pass duty, including the pH~10 feed.

\subsection{Implications for regeneration}
\flag{link points to section \ref{sec:fouling-ip}}

The two membrane are expected to respond differently to a new IP layer. 

For 4238C, the surface is mostly clear of foulants and deposits, but the new PA layer has to cover existing holes in the PA layer. The change in surface charge and hydrophilicity can also affect how the surface takes up the aqueous monomer as compared with traditional IP methods.

For 4297B, the deposits are more likely to affect IP. Iron oxide and silica particles here did not appear in the extraction sample from caustic soda and acid, but do appear on SEM-EDS analysis, suggesting the compounds arn't easily broken down by acid and base cleans. The biological film can also change the surface that the new layer forms on. As a result, it is likely that despite extensive cleaning of membranes, these foulants still remain right up till the IP process of regeneration.

In Campaign~2, PWP was higher for the mine-site element than for 4238C (0.330 against \qty{0.208}{LMH.bar^{-1}}; Welch's $t$-test, $p = 0.028$), while rejection did not differ significantly (81.9 against \qty{76.6}{\percent}, $p = 0.31$). \subsection{Comparison with element test}
4238A testing becomes a useful check, because the EoL element still met the specification as an element (\qty{99.65}{\percent}) rejection. During coupon testing however, the \flag{What was tested PWP?} PWP was only measured to be \qty{0.082}{LMH.bar^{-1}} and a rejection of \qty{78.1}{\percent}, which is 21 percentage points below the element testing results. This gap is similar for the damaged 4238C (about 21~pp). This means taht the difference between coupon and element performance cannot simply be explained by the damage to 4238C. 

For 4238C, the values are much lower than those measured by Membrane Works on the full elements. Their test gave a flux of \qty{71.9}{LMH} at about \qty{10}{bar}, or roughly \qty{7}{LMH.bar^{-1}}, with a rejection of \qty{97.3}{\percent} \citep{MembraneWorks2025Desal}. The coupon permeability is about 35 times lower, and the rejection about 21 percentage points lower. The permeability measured in this experiment is about 21 times lower than the rated value for a new ESPA2-MAX element (about \qty{4.4}{LMH.bar^{-1}}). 

For 4297B membranes, no testing was done on the RO element, and the reported drop to around \qty{85}{\percent} was measured for the whole RO train \citep{MembraneWorks2026Mine}, so there are no element values to compare with. 

\subsection{Why coupon results differ}
Testing using the 8-inch RO module from Membrane Works revealed a much higher permeability and rejection, at \qty{71.9}{LMH} flux, 97.3\% rejection at 10 bar and \qty{1500}{\milli\gram\per\litre}. That experiment used a second-pass ESPA2-MAX element which was reported to be underperforming for desalination purposes. The flux measured on site of that element was double the expected \qty{37.3}{LMH}, ane below the expected 99.46\%, likely due to pinhole damage in the PA layer. Laboratory flat-sheet dye-cell test also gave a rejection of only 61\%, compared with 91-92\% for other coupons tested.
4238A however shows that this explanation is incomplete, were performance as an element yielded \qty{99.65}{\percent} on the rig, essentially the same as the damaged 4238C (\qty{76.6}{\percent}). Coupon PWP was also 15-35 times lower than the corresponding elements for all three membranes. Two things could have contributed to this difference. First is the idea of apparent rejection, where at constant salt permeability, the observed rejection falls as water flux falls. Coupon fluxes of \qtyrange{0.5}{6}{LMH} are an order of manitude below the element fluxes, so much lower observed rejection is expected (given no change to salt permeability). It is also possible that salt could have leaked through via local defects or O-ring seal defects. A small coupon area of \qty{0.0139}{\square\metre} makes this leak more apparent. The small coupon size also means local defects in coupon is amplified greatly, rather than averaged out like it would for an element testing. 

Because this offset applied to every condition tested on the rig, comparisons between the conditions are valid, but these values should not be compared to the element or datasheet values as there is clearly a big disparity between them.

\section{Cleaning outcomes}
\label{sec:res-cleaning}
This section reports what the cleaning steps removed from each membrane. As-received coupons were compared with extensive clean coupons, as described in Section~\ref{sec:cleaning}. The effect of cleaning on regeneration is covered in Section~\ref{sec:fouling-ip}.

\subsection{Fouling profiles and expected cleaning response}
\flag{Repeats alot of what was said in 4.1. Cross refernce to section \ref{sec:res-fouling}}\\
The autopsies give a clear idea of what cleaning could remove.

For 4238C, there was little deposit across the membrane. The membrane was visually clean, and SEM-EDS found scattered iron and aluminium oxide, biological particles and salt crystals. Cleaning was therefore not expected to produce a large visible change. The main issues found with 4238C were the holes in the PA layer and changes in surface chemistry and charge, which cleaning cannot repair.

For 4297B, the deposit comprised different elements and compounds, all expected to react to cleaning differently.
\paragraph{Calcium, magnesium and sulphate.} These made up most of the extractable foulant and were recovered by acid and caustic extraction in the fouling extraction test conducted by Membrane Works. They were expected to be removed in a similar fashion by acid clean. EDTA, which binds metal ions such as calcium and magnesium, was expected to help as its chelating ability increases at alkaline pH where more of its carboxylic groups are deprotonated \citep{elimelech2005chemical}.
\paragraph{Organic and biological materials.} The extractable carbon and biofilm seen with Astra Blue staining were expected to be removed mainly by the caustic clean. However, where organic foulants are cross-linked by calcium, NaOH alone has been shown to give poor flux recovery, while EDTA was considerably more effective by breaking the calcium bridges \citep{elimelech2005chemical}.
\paragraph{Iron oxide.} \flag{Need to reevaluate, cannot assume no iron oxide cuz no colour change} Almost no iron was recovered by acid and caustic extraction, even though iron oxide was clearly visible in SEM-EDS. The iron oxide was therefore not expected to be fully removed by HCl and NaOH cleaning.
\paragraph{Silica particles.} These were identified by Membrane Works as sand rather than scale, and were not expected to dissolve in either solution.\\

Additionally, Membrane Works conducted a cleaning study supporting a lot of the points above. A high pH biological and clay cleaner showed some improvements to the fouling profile with the advanced iron cleaning and specialty silica cleaning showing similar results. Increasing the advanced iron cleaner from \qty{2}{\percent} to \qty{4}{\percent} removed more of the fouling, but some remained. Since specialist iron cleaners did not fully remove the iron, the HCl and NaOH cleans here were expected to leave iron oxide on 4297B.

Change in PWP or salt rejection can be cause by the cleaning itself, rather than simply foulant removal. \citet{tu2015chemical} evaluated this effect by soaking virgin ESPA2 low pressure RO membranes in cleaning solutions for \qty{25}{\hour} at \qty{30}{\degreeCelsius}. This is expected to represent the cumulative cleaning over about three years of plant operation. \subsection{Visual observations}
\paragraph{Desal-site membrane 4238C. } 
Cleaning caused no visible change to 4238C. Before cleaning, the polyamide (feed side) was a shiny, light yellow colour, and the polyester (permeate side) was a dull, matte white. After both the acid and base cleans, the coupon looked exactly the same. The acid and base solutions also showed no colour change after use. This suggests that little foulant was dissolved into the solutions, which is expected according to the autopsy as only light deposits were seen.

\paragraph{Mine-site membrane 4297B.}
The colour of 4297B did not change noticeably after cleaning. \flag{The acid and base solutions also showed no colour change, which indicates that the iron oxide was not dissolved. This agrees with the autopsy, where acid and caustic extraction recovered almost no iron.}

The main observation for 4297B was delamination along the edges of the coupons (Figure~\ref{fig:4297B-delam}). In these areas the PA layer had begun to peel away from the polysulfone support and could be lifted off with very little force. The damage was first seen immediately after the base clean and appeared again in repeated experiments.


The autopsy found no delamination damage on the EoL 4297B elements, so the damage was not there before cleaning. However, 4238C showed no delamination under the same cleaning conditions, so the cleaning process along was not enough to cause it. 

The base clean reached pH values up to 11.5. This is slightly above the manufacturer's limit for continuous operation (pH~2--11) but within the short-term (\qty{30}{\minute}) cleaning range (pH~1--13) \citep{MembraneWorks2026Mine}. Damage to membranes from cleaning outside the recommended pH range is discussed in Section~\ref{sec:res-cleaning-chem}. Since 4238C was not affected at the same pH, the more likely explanation is that the bond between the PA and the support in 4297B had already weakened during service. The autopsy found that foulants had penetrated into the support layers of 4297B, and indentations left on the surface by past scale may also have weakened the layers.

The damage was concentrated along the coupon edges. This suggests that cutting the coupon exposed the interface between the layers, so the cleaning solution could get in between them.

Delaminated areas have no PA layer, so they would be expected to behave like a UF/MF membrane (high permeability, low rejection) if they were included in performance testing. The damaged edges were therefore trimmed off and discarded before testing.

  For the mine-site membrane (4297B), the six extensive clean coupons had fairly consistent flux readings. The flux ranged from \qty{3.79}{LMH} to \qty{7.22}{LMH}, giving a mean of 5.60 $\pm$ \qty{1.41}{LMH}. Rejection varied significantly from \qty{64.2}{\percent} to \qty{91.2}{\percent}, mainly because two coupons tested yielded a much lower rejection than others. 

  The desal site membrane showed much more variation between the tests. Across seven coupons, the mean flux was 4.55 $\pm$ \qty{4.42}{LMH}, with a relative standard deviation of about \qty{97}{\percent}. This is a huge spread of readings, but does not come from pure measurement error. Taking repeated readings on the same coupon differed by at most \qty{0.34}{LMH}, while coupons had a mean flux of \qty{0.67}{LMH} to \qty{11.76}{LMH}. Most of the spread came from coupon sample 5 and 6 (C5 and C6 from hereon), where they were measured to have a flux of \qty{9.86}{LMH} and \qty{11.76}{LMH}. These were three to six times higher than the other five coupons, and the rejection of these two coupons were also the lowest measured at \qty{69.2}{\percent} and \qty{67.1}{\percent}.\\

  These two coupons should not be treated as measurment outliers. Rig or test-day effect can also be ruled out, as C7 was tested in the same session at the same pressure, and gave a result close to others (\qty{3.86}{LMH}, \qty{82.4}{\percent}). 4238C also showed no signs of delamination, and permeability readings were taken in ~15 mins interval with no signs of significant change, ruling out compaction damage. The two coupons had both high flux and low rejection, which suggests that feed water leaked through openings in the PA layer. 

  The membrane autopsy found similar damage for this membrane. SEM showed holes in PA layer, and the dye test showed dense spots and streaks of dye spots spread unevenly across the permeate side as seen in Figure~\ref{fig:dye_test_full} \citep{MembraneWorks2025Desal}. C5 and 6 were cut from the same section of the membrane sheet, so they likely came from regions with a cluster of these pinholes. As the tested membrane coupons are small (\qty{0.0139}{\square\metre}), these small, local defects can dominate readings in a way that wouldn't happen with full membrane elements. It is highly likely therefore that C5 and 6 are results from a damaged part of the membrane, and do not represent the effect of cleaning. 
  
  Without C5 and 6 coupons, the extensive clean 4238C membranes have a mean flux of 2.04 $\pm$ \qty{1.16}{LMH}, a PWP of 0.13 $\pm$ \qty{0.08}{LMH.bar^{-1}} and a rejection of 84.3 $\pm$ \qty{6.9}{\percent} across 5 coupons. The relative standard deviation for flux fell from \qty{97}{\percent} to \qty{57}{\percent}. There is still some spread that remains, but none of the other coupons showed a high-flux low rejection pattern. The values without C5 and 6 will be used to assess the effect of cleaning and to compare against IP.\\

  \flag{}
  Two of the extensive clean 4297B coupons gave low rejection (\qty{64.3}{\percent} and \qty{69.3}{\percent}). These were also the two highest-flux coupons (\qty{7.22}{LMH} and \qty{7.13}{LMH}), about 1.5 times the mean of the remaining four (\qty{4.81}{LMH}). This is a similar but much weaker pattern than C5 and C6, whose flux was around five times that of the other 4238C coupons. Unlike 4238C, there is no independent evidence of holes in the 4297B PA layer: SEM showed indentations from removed scale rather than holes, and no dye test was performed \citep{MembraneWorks2026Mine}. Higher flux combined with lower rejection is instead consistent with a looser PA layer in these regions, which may reflect local variation in the chlorine damage detected by XPS. These coupons were therefore retained. Excluding them, extensive clean 4297B gave a flux of $4.81 \pm 0.90$\,\unit{LMH} and a rejection of $87.3 \pm 5.5$\,\unit{\percent}, which is considered in Section~\ref{sec:res-cleaning-compare}.
