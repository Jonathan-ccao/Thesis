\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}


\section{Background and motivation}
\label{sec:background}
Reverse osmosis (RO) is now the dominant desalination technology, and as of 2019, the world's 15,906 desalination plants together produced around 95 million \unit{\cubic\metre} of water per day \citep{Jones2019}. Most plants rely on spiral-wound thin-film composite (TFC) membrane elements that degrade with use. RO elements typically remain in service for five to nine years \citep{Ding_2026,Lawler2015}, and an estimated \qtyrange{5}{20}{\percent} of installed membranes are replaced each year \citep{BerattoRamos2024,Guclu2022}. As water demand grows, the volume of end-of-life (EoL) membranes grows with it. In this thesis, an EoL membrane is one that has been removed from service because fouling or chemical degradation means it no longer meets its required specifications.

Almost all of these elements go to landfill or incineration \citep{Khanzada2024}. A module is almost entirely polymeric and barely degrades in landfill, while incineration recovers some energy but emits a lot of \ce{CO2} \citep{Lawler2015}. Both routes discard an element whose polyester (PET) and polysulfone (PSf) layers are usually still intact \citep{Molinaa2018}. Most of the damage occurs in the polyamide (PA) layer, which is only about \qtyrange{0.1}{0.2}{\micro\metre} thick but provides almost all of the membrane's salt rejection \citep{LejarazuLarranaga2022}. If that layer could be restored, more elements could be returned to service rather than discarded.


\section{Problem statement}
\label{sec:problem}
Current alternatives to disposal do not restore RO performance. Direct reuse only deferes disposal \citep{Lawler2015}, and NaOCl conversion to nanofiltration (NF) or ultrafiltration (UF) permanently gives up RO selectivity \citep{GarciaPacheco2015,Molinaa2018}. Regenerating the PA layer by interfacial polymerisation (IP) has only been shown in two studies \citep{Ogbe2025,Ogbe2026}, on either NaOCl-stripped membranes or mildly degraded elements. It is not yet known how elements from other sources respond, or whether a routine chemical clean would help or hinder IP. 


\section{Research aim}
\label{sec:aim}
This research aims to evaluate whether EoL RO membranes can be regenerated by depositing a new PA layer via ex-situ IP, without stripping the old layer, and how fouling and cleaning pre-treatment affect the membrane's permeability and salt rejection.

\section{Objectives}
\label{sec:objectives}
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item Characterise three EoL RO elements (4238A, 4238C and 4297B) and their fouling profiles.
  \item Prepare the membranes as received, after a commercial clean, or after an extensive clean.
  \item Regenerate the PA layer by ex-situ IP.
  \item Measure pure water permeability (PWP) and NaCl rejection, and calculate the salt permeability coefficient $B$.
  \item Autopsy selected coupons to relate surface condition to performance.
\end{enumerate}



\section{Scope and limitations}
\label{sec:scope}
Testing was done at coupon scale over two campaigns. Campaign~1 (4238A, commercial clean) served as methods development, and Campaign~2 (4238C and 4297B, extensive clean) provided the main results. NaOCl stripping, optimisation of IP or cleaning chemistry, module-scale trials, life cycle assessments and techno-economic analysis are out of scope. The main constraints are limited replicates from element sheets and unquantified concentration polarisation (Section~\ref{sec:res-limitations}).
